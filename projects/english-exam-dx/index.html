English Exam Content Digitization

Project Manager · April 2025 – August 2025 · Dahm-won (Abby) Heo

Situation

The client contracted us to digitize a 200K+-item English exam question bank. The operation was up and running before I arrived, and under the existing design, about 100K items had been tagged, half the total scope.

I joined as a PM while the project was mid-flight. There was a co-PM, though she had started only a few days ahead of me, so neither of us had much history with it. The departing PM spent a few days with us, mostly walking through the worker tagging guide. It covered how each page should be labeled, but not how the project was run. Soon after, the team lead announced she was leaving, and by the time the first delivery was due, she was gone. That left the two of us and a single data engineer holding a system none of us had designed and barely had time to learn.

The first delivery carried the batch tagged under the previous lead's design. It came back with a verdict. The client called about half of it unusable and raised the possibility of damages.

Diagnosis

Once I understood how the system worked, two problems stood out.

The first was integrity. The platform captured everything about a question except its original number, so nothing tied a question to its answer sheet. On delivery days, the engineer and PMs compared entire PDFs by hand just to confirm the question and answer counts matched. There was another risk. If a student saw the wrong answer or explanation on a question they had solved, it would damage the client's credibility with its own users.

The second was capacity. Answer-sheet tagging had never been built into the worker tasks. Instead, the previous PM and the one who had joined days before me were copying answer entries into Excel by hand. That could never scale.

Speeding up delivery would mean nothing if answers still landed on the wrong questions, so integrity came first and capacity second.

Approach

Integrity

The obvious fix was to have workers tag the question numbers themselves. But with 100K items already tagged, that would have required rebuilding the platform and redoing the completed work, which the timeline could not absorb. Instead, I used data the platform was logging. Its export recorded the order each item was processed in, both across the full PDF and within each page, though it did not retain the printed question numbers.

That processing order worked as a surrogate identifier, a stand-in for the missing number. It did not align exactly with the printed numbering, because duplicates and out-of-scope items were skipped during processing. Combined with each workbook's item counts, however, it was enough to link a question to its answer and locate a mismatch at the specific page and position. This replaced the manual PDF comparison the team had relied on.

With that link established, I could address the next question, whether the output rendered correctly on the client's side. I asked the platform team to build a QC viewer, but they declined due to limited resources. I built one myself with Cursor AI. As a PM, I did not have access to the company's GitHub account, so I hosted it on my own and kept it browser-only, which meant the data never left the local machine. From then on, we pulled a sample from each delivery and reviewed it in the viewer before it went out, which caught rendering defects that raw JSON would have hidden. One issue still affected both fixes, but it could wait until the immediate problems were resolved.

Capacity

Answer sheets varied by publisher, including single and two-column pages, boxed numbers, and mixed answer-and-explanation formats. OCR alone was not sufficient. Each entry had to be converted into a structured format that separated the answer from its explanation and recorded which column it sat in. Tesseract, an open-source text-recognition engine, could read the text but could not reliably separate those fields or preserve the layout.

Gemini Pro Vision handled this more reliably. I called it through its API with a prompt I designed to return each entry as structured fields, and I grouped similar formats into batches so one prompt could cover a whole set. Reviewers checked the first pass, and their corrections fed back in two ways. Some sharpened the prompt. Others reshaped the output schema itself, such as splitting the answer into separate fields for multiple-choice numbers and written responses. The manual copying the two PMs had been doing became a repeatable pipeline.

Source control

The remaining issue was the source files. Once integrity and capacity were stable, I addressed it. No one had documented the basic conditions of each source file, so a given defect could originate from either a worker error or a flawed file, with no way to distinguish the two. I started recording which workbooks had been set up on the platform, what resolution their PDFs were converted to PNG at, and whether a file was locked for editing. With that record, we could separate the two causes and return to the client with specific evidence when a file was responsible. Reliable delivery of 200K+ items depended on controlling the source.

What Changed

The fixes landed in two stages. In the first, the surrogate identifier let the team reconcile question and answer counts at the workbook and page level, rather than searching across entire outputs by hand. The answer-sheet pipeline was improving in parallel, as reviewer corrections fed back into the prompts and schema. After this round, usable output rose from below 50% to around 85%.

The second stage closed the rest of the gap. The browser QC viewer and source tracking caught rendering and source-file problems before each delivery, whereas earlier they had surfaced only in client feedback. From that point, our sample checks found no material defects, and the client raised no further complaints. The damages threat was dropped.

Framing

My role sat between operations and data quality. Workers handled the tagging and a single engineer owned the JSON pipeline, so I could not rely on a team to build tooling. I worked from the data the platform already produced, and when the platform team declined to build a QC viewer, I built one myself. Besides the tools, the order of the work carried its own weight. A faster pipeline would not have helped while the answers were still landing on the wrong questions. That ordering is what moved the project from the first delivery's failure to a stable process.
